# How the forced-choice format works

Most personality questionnaires you have seen ask you to rate statements on a scale, from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The OPQ32r does not. It shows statements in small sets and makes you choose between them. This page explains how that works, why SHL uses it, and how to handle it calmly.

## What you see on screen

SHL's candidate example page describes the steps:

1. Three statements about behaviour at work appear together.
2. You select the one that is **most like you**.
3. That statement disappears.
4. From the two that remain, you select the one that is **most like you**.

That is all. The third statement is, by elimination, the least like you of the three.

SHL's 2019 article on the redesign explains that the older format asked you to pick both a "most like me" and a "least like me" statement. The current one asks a single question twice, "select the statement that describes you best", which SHL says makes it up to 50% faster to complete.

## A worked example

Here is a block written for these notes. It is not from the OPQ32r.

| | Statement | Scale it points to |
|---|---|---|
| A | I plan my work weeks in advance. | Forward Thinking |
| B | I enjoy winning people round to my point of view. | Persuasive |
| C | I stay calm when things go wrong. | Relaxed |

Suppose you are a recent graduate who does keep a weekly plan, rarely tries to sell ideas to others, and gets a little tense when things go wrong.

- **First choice:** A. You can point to the weekly plan on your desk.
- **Second choice:** between B and C. You think of your last group project. When the deadline slipped, you were tense but kept going. You did not try to convince anyone of anything. Neither feels strong, so you ask which one a teammate would say about you. Probably neither, but "calm" is closer than "persuasive". You pick C.
- **Result:** A first, C second, B third.

Someone else, a salesperson who hates planning, would honestly rank B first and A last. Both answers are correct for the person giving them.

## Why SHL uses forced choice

SHL's psychometric testing page says single-statement rating scales are "often considered as transparent and easy to fake", and that the OPQ's forced-choice design, with three equally desirable statements, is meant to reduce faking and bias, because it is not clear which statement would be considered "best".

What that means for you:

- **You cannot say yes to everything.** On a rating scale, you could agree strongly with every positive statement. Here, choosing one thing always means not choosing another.
- **There is no safe statement.** All three are designed to sound reasonable. Trying to guess the "right" one wastes time and tends to produce a profile that does not hang together.
- **It feels uncomfortable, and that is normal.** Many blocks will offer two things that are both true of you. Pick the one that is more true and move on.

## How choices become scores

Each statement is written to reflect one of the 32 scales. In a forced-choice design, what counts is the pattern of your choices across the whole questionnaire, not any single block, so one awkward choice does not define your profile.

SHL's sample profile reports show the result as a sten score from 1 to 10 for each scale, compared with a comparison group. SHL does not set out its scoring model on its public candidate pages, so we do not describe it further. See [how results are reported](scoring.md).

## Handling hard blocks

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| All three statements are true of you | Pick the one you do most often, or the one others notice most |
| None of the three is true of you | Pick the one that is least untrue. You still have to choose. |
| One statement is true at work, another at home | Answer about work only |
| You are tempted to pick what the job ad seems to want | Pick what you actually do. See [answering honestly](answering-honestly.md). |
| You have been stuck for over 30 seconds | Go with your first reaction and move on |

## Practise the format

Ten minutes with real blocks makes the format feel normal. Work through the example blocks in the three area guides, starting with [Relationships with People](sections/relationships-with-people.md), then try the blocks on the free [SHL OPQ32 practice page on PrepClubs](https://prepclubs.com/free-practice-tests/shl-opq32?utm_source=github&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=study-notes&utm_content=shl-opq32r).
